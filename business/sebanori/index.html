FRONTIER AI+ADAPTIVE LEARNING+HUMAN MENTORSHIP

01 / THE EVERYDAY OPPORTUNITY

Training shouldn’t mean
starting from scratch.

How do you turn “let me show you” into a learning path a new colleague can follow?

Receiving a delivery or stocking a shelf can look simple until you’re new. An experienced colleague knows what to check, when to ask for help and what needs supervised practice. Repeating every explanation takes them away from their own work.

A mentor captures that know-how in a short explanation. Optional AI drafting helps organize it into a learning path. The mentor checks the steps and approves the plan. A trainee gets a focus for practice and can reflect or ask a question afterward.

WHAT MAKES IT DIFFERENT

Three parts.
One learning relationship.

Frontier AI + Adaptive Learning + Human Mentorship. AI helps prepare the path; the mentor and trainee put it into practice and adjust it together.

Frontier AI

Turn a mentor’s explanation into a draft learning path. Optional drafting uses models from OpenAI or Anthropic, the makers of ChatGPT and Claude. The mentor checks the content before it is used.

Adaptive Learning

Start with what this person can do: not yet, with help, or independently. The mentor uses observed practice and questions to choose the next step and adjust the support.

Human Mentorship

Your experienced people remain the teachers. They demonstrate the work, review AI suggestions and decide when a trainee is ready for more independence.

This is the product’s design approach, not a validated training method. AI provider names identify optional technology, not partnerships or endorsements.

SUPPORT THAT CHANGES WITH PRACTICE

The same task.
A different next step.

Two new colleagues may need different support with receiving a delivery. The mentor observes each person and agrees what to practise next.

  1. Not yet

    The trainee watches a demonstration and asks questions.

  2. With help

    The trainee practises alongside the mentor, who can prompt or step in.

  3. Independently

    The mentor has recorded that the trainee can do this skill on their own within the agreed scope. They can still ask for help.

The mentor updates the skill mark and adjusts the next practice step. This is mentor-guided adaptation; automatic personalization has not been established. A mark is not a safety certification or permission to operate equipment.

Why this approach? A short explanation of Vygotsky’s ZPD

The design is inspired by Lev Vygotsky’s Zone of Proximal Development: the difference between what someone can do independently and what they can do with guidance. In plain terms, offer a next step they can tackle with help, then reduce support as they become ready.

Our three support levels are a practical design choice inspired by that idea, not a validated assessment tool or a claim that this product has been scientifically proven.

Background: L. S. Vygotsky, Mind in Society (1978), chapter 6, page 86 (PDF). The original discussion concerns children’s learning; applying the idea to this workplace prototype is a design adaptation.

Required hands-on safety instruction, workplace procedures and authorized supervision still apply. AI cannot authorize equipment operation or decide that someone is safe to work alone.

03 / THE HUGONOMY THREAD

Build capability.
Keep agency.

Explore Hugonomy’s philosophy ↗

People should remain active participants in the work they do with AI.

Hugonomy’s wider work asks how technology can support human awareness, judgment and intentional action. SEBANORI brings that question into learning at work.

The ambition is practical: help a mentor prepare a better learning path, give the trainee room to ask questions and practise, and keep approval grounded in human observation.

Human augmentation is the design goal. Whether SEBANORI improves learning in real workplaces is a question to test, not an outcome already established.

WHAT A PILOT WOULD NEED TO ANSWER

Useful for the team?
Let’s measure that.

With an agreed plan and appropriate permissions, a future pilot could compare a small set of tasks before and during a trial:

  • How much mentor time goes into repeated explanations and preparing learning plans?
  • Can trainees find the next step, ask for help and explain what they have learned?
  • What can trainees demonstrate with help, and then independently, as observed by the mentor?
  • Do mentors find the AI drafts accurate and worth reviewing? Does the tool fit short check-ins during real work?

Agree the observations, time period and success criteria beforehand. Account for differences in tasks and experience, and include mentor preparation time. These are proposed evaluation questions, not results or built-in analytics.

No completed pilots, paying customers, verified cost savings or proven productivity improvements are being claimed. Workplace testing requires secure infrastructure, privacy controls and agreement with the people involved.
